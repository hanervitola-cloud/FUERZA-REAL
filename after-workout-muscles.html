What Happens Inside Your Muscles After a Workout (Hour by Hour)

7 min read · Recovery
← All articles
Written by Haner Vitola · Updated October 2026

The workout itself doesn't build muscle. It's the signal. The building happens afterward — over the next hours and days — and what you do during that time decides how much of your effort actually turns into results.

In summary
  • Training fatigues and slightly damages the muscle; growth happens during recovery
  • Muscle protein synthesis rises within hours and can stay elevated for one to two days
  • Soreness usually peaks one to three days after training, and it doesn't measure growth
  • Protein, carbohydrates and sleep in the following hours and days decide how well you recover
  • Ice baths right after lifting may reduce your long-term muscle gains

From the Earned Strength YouTube channel: What Happens Inside Your Muscles After a Workout (Hour by Hour)

Training is the signal, recovery is where you grow

Lifting weights creates mechanical tension in the muscle, uses up stored energy, and causes small amounts of damage to muscle fibers. Right after a hard session you're actually weaker than when you started.

The adaptation — more muscle, more strength — happens while you recover. That's why two people can do the same workout and get very different results: what happens after the gym matters as much as what happens in it.

The timeline, hour by hour

0–1 hours after

Fatigue and the pump fade

  • Your muscles are temporarily weaker: part of their stored glycogen (carbohydrate) has been used, and fatigue lowers how much force they can produce.
  • The "pump" — the swelling from extra blood and fluid in the muscle — fades within the next hour or so. It feels good, but it isn't growth.
  • Muscle protein synthesis, the process that builds new muscle proteins, starts to rise.
1–4 hours after

The building process speeds up

  • Muscle protein synthesis is clearly elevated. In a classic study (MacDougall et al., 1995), it was about 50% above normal four hours after a heavy workout.
  • This is a good time for a meal with protein and carbohydrates — but you don't have to rush. The "anabolic window" is much wider than the old 30-minute rule (more in the protein guide).
Around 24 hours after

Peak building, energy refilled

  • In the same study, muscle protein synthesis was more than double the normal rate 24 hours after training — about 109% higher.
  • With normal meals that include carbohydrates, most of the glycogen used in a typical lifting session is usually restored within about a day.
24–72 hours after

Soreness peaks

  • Delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS) usually peaks one to three days after training and fades within about a week (Cheung et al., 2003).
  • It's most noticeable after new exercises, a jump in volume, or coming back from a break — not necessarily after your most productive sessions.
36–48 hours and beyond

Back toward baseline

  • In trained lifters, muscle protein synthesis returned close to normal by about 36 hours (MacDougall et al., 1995).
  • In people new to training, it stayed elevated for up to 48 hours (Phillips et al., 1997).
  • This is one reason many programs train each muscle about twice a week: a new signal arrives as the previous one fades.

In the first weeks, a lot of it is repair

Here's something most people don't know: when you're new to training, much of that protein-building goes into repairing damaged muscle rather than adding new muscle. A study by Damas and colleagues (2016) found that muscle protein synthesis only started to line up with real muscle growth after about three weeks of training — once the muscle damage from each session had gone down.

That's also why feeling destroyed after every workout isn't a good sign. The more damage you cause, the more of your recovery goes into fixing it instead of growing.

From my own training

For a long time I took every single set to failure, and recovery dragged on for days — once, my biceps stayed sore for two weeks. That's a clear example of what this timeline describes: with that much damage, recovery goes into repair instead of growth. I wrote more about it in Signs of Overtraining.

What to do in the hours and days after training

01

Eat a meal with protein within a few hours

A good target is roughly 0.4 g of protein per kg of bodyweight per meal (Schoenfeld and Aragon, 2018) — about 28 g for someone weighing 70 kg. Your total for the day still matters most.

02

Don't skip the carbohydrates

Carbs refill the glycogen you used, which matters most if you train again the next day. Rice, potatoes, fruit, bread or oats with your post-workout meal are enough.

03

Sleep like it's part of the program

One night without sleep reduced muscle protein synthesis by 18% in a controlled study (Lamon et al., 2021). More in Sleep and Recovery.

04

Skip the ice bath right after lifting

In a study by Roberts et al. (2015), people who regularly took cold-water baths after strength training gained less muscle and strength over 12 weeks than people who did light active recovery instead. Cold can help with soreness, but not with growth.

05

Keep moving lightly

You don't need total rest between sessions. Walking and normal daily activity are fine and can make you feel less stiff.

Does soreness mean the workout worked?

No. Soreness mostly reflects how new or unusual the training was, not how much muscle you'll build. Many of your best sessions won't leave you sore at all, especially once your body is used to a routine. If you want the full explanation, it's the first myth in 8 Gym Myths That Are Holding You Back.
