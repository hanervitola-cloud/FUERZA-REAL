High-Protein Foods: Which Ones Are Actually Worth It (and the Label Tricks)

6 min read · Nutrition
← All articles
Written by the creator of Earned Strength · Updated October 2026

Plenty of foods contain protein. Far fewer are actually good protein sources. The difference comes down to one number most people never look at: how much protein you get for the calories you eat.

In summary
  • What matters is protein per calorie, not just grams of protein
  • Lean meats, fish, eggs and Greek yogurt are dense protein sources; peanut butter and nuts aren't
  • In the EU, a food can only be labeled "high protein" if at least 20% of its calories come from protein
  • Labels use serving sizes, collagen and "protein" versions of snacks to look better than they are
  • Use the free checker below with any nutrition label

The number that matters: protein per calorie

Looking only at grams of protein can fool you. 100 g of peanut butter has almost as much protein as 100 g of chicken breast — but more than three times the calories. If you're trying to build muscle without overeating, that difference is everything.

There are two easy ways to measure it:

01

Grams of protein per 100 calories

Divide the grams of protein by the calories and multiply by 100. Chicken breast gives you about 19 g of protein per 100 calories; peanut butter, about 4 g.

02

Percentage of calories from protein

Each gram of protein has about 4 calories. Multiply the grams of protein by 4, divide by the total calories, and multiply by 100. That's the number food regulators use for "high protein" claims.

Why peanut butter isn't a protein food

Peanut butter has protein, but about three-quarters of its calories come from fat, and only around 15–17% come from protein. Two tablespoons (32 g) give you roughly 7–8 g of protein for about 190 calories.

To get the protein of one 150 g chicken breast — around 45 g — you'd need about six servings of peanut butter, which is over 1,100 calories. That doesn't make it a bad food: it's a good source of healthy fats. It's just not where most of your protein should come from.

Label tricks to watch for

01

"High protein" doesn't always mean what you think

In the European Union, Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006 only allows the "high protein" claim if at least 20% of a food's calories come from protein, and "source of protein" if at least 12% do. In the United States, the FDA's "high in protein" claim is based on grams per serving — at least 10 g — so a high-calorie snack can still qualify.

02

Tiny serving sizes

A small serving makes calories and sugar look low next to the protein. Check how many servings you actually eat.

03

Collagen counted as protein

Collagen counts toward the protein number on the label, but it's an incomplete protein — it lacks the essential amino acid tryptophan (Paul et al., 2019) and is low in leucine — so it's less useful for building muscle than the label suggests.

04

"Protein" versions of snacks

Protein cereals, chips, cookies and bars often add a few grams of protein to a food that's still mostly carbs and fat. Compare them using protein per 100 calories, not the front of the box.

05

Numbers "with milk"

Some cereals show protein for a serving prepared with milk. A good part of that protein comes from the milk, not the cereal.

My experience

Most of my protein comes from chicken, meat and eggs — which, looking at the table above, are among the most protein-dense foods you can buy. I don't use protein powder (here's what I think about it) and I don't count grams. Choosing foods that are dense in protein is what makes not counting work.

How to build meals around protein

01

Anchor every meal with a dense protein

Chicken, fish, lean meat, eggs or Greek yogurt in each main meal gets you most of the way to your daily target. See the example day in the protein guide.

02

Use beans and lentils as support

They add protein and fiber, but on their own they're less dense than animal sources, so pair them with something else.

03

Count nuts and peanut butter as fats

Enjoy them, but think of them as your healthy-fat portion, not your protein portion.
