What Happens to Your Muscles When You Stop Training

7 min read · Training
← All articles
Written by the creator of Earned Strength · Updated October 2026

Missing a week of training feels like losing everything. It isn't. Muscle is far more stubborn than it looks in the mirror after a few days off — and the research on what actually happens when you stop is mostly good news.

In summary
  • In the first days, muscles look flatter mostly because of lost water and glycogen, not lost muscle
  • Strength is largely maintained for about three weeks without training
  • Very little training is enough to keep most of your muscle
  • An injury that immobilizes a limb causes much faster loss than simply skipping the gym
  • Muscle you lose comes back faster than it took to build the first time

The first week: why you look smaller (but aren't)

When you stop training, the first thing to drop isn't muscle tissue — it's the glycogen and water stored inside your muscles. Glycogen is stored together with water, so when there's less of it, the muscle looks flatter and you may even lose a little weight on the scale.

That change is fast in both directions: within a few days of training and eating normally again, it comes right back. If you've ever felt "small" after a short vacation, this is almost certainly what you were seeing.

Weeks two and three: strength holds steady

A meta-analysis by Bosquet and colleagues (2013), which combined many studies on stopping training, found that maximal strength was largely maintained for about the first three weeks without training and only declined after that.

Another study shows how little a short break really costs. Ogasawara and colleagues (2013) compared people who trained continuously for 24 weeks with people who trained in cycles of six weeks on and three weeks off. By the end of the study, both groups had gained similar amounts of muscle and strength.

What this means

A week or two off — for exams, a trip or being sick — won't undo your progress. It might even help you come back fresher.

After a month and longer

Past roughly four weeks without any training, losses become more noticeable. Strength and muscle size gradually decline, and endurance fitness tends to drop faster than strength (Mujika and Padilla, 2000).

How fast it happens depends on how long you'd been training, your age, how much you eat, and whether you're still moving around normally. Staying active in daily life slows the process down.

When the break is an injury

Not training and not using a muscle at all are very different situations. In a study where healthy young men had one leg immobilized in a cast, they lost about 3.5% of their thigh muscle size in just five days (Wall et al., 2014). That's why injuries feel so much worse than a normal break.

There is something that can help, though. In a study by Farthing and colleagues (2009), people who had one arm immobilized and kept training the other arm lost much less strength in the immobilized arm than people who didn't train at all. This effect is called cross-education. Always check with your doctor or physiotherapist before training around an injury.

My experience: this is where I am right now

I had to stop training because of injuries that had nothing to do with the gym, and I've lost a lot of the progress I worked for. If you're in the same situation, the next two sections are the most important part of this article: how little it takes to hold on to what you have, and why what you've lost comes back faster.

How little it takes to keep your muscle

If you can still train a little, you need far less than you think. In a study by Bickel, Cross and Bamman (2011), young adults first trained three times a week for 16 weeks. Then they dropped to a single session per week, with a fraction of the original volume, for 32 weeks. They kept their muscle size and strength — even the group doing just one-ninth of their original training volume.

Practical takeaway

During a busy or limited period, even one solid session per week for each muscle group can keep most of what you've built.

The good news: muscle memory

When a muscle grows, its cells gain extra nuclei. Animal research (Bruusgaard et al., 2010) found that those nuclei stay even after the muscle shrinks from lack of use. In humans, Seaborne and colleagues (2018) found that muscle keeps an "epigenetic memory" of previous growth: after training, stopping, and training again, the muscles grew more during the second round.

In practice, regaining lost muscle is usually much faster than building it the first time. I go deeper into this, and into how to come back step by step, in Why Motivation Isn't Enough (and Discipline Is).

What to do during a break

01

Keep your protein up

Your muscles still need protein to hold on to what you've built. Use the ranges in the protein guide as a reference.

02

Stay active in whatever way you can

Walking, mobility work and training uninjured areas (with your doctor's approval) all slow down the loss.

03

Do the minimum if you can

One short session a week is far better than nothing. It keeps both your muscle and your habit alive.

04

Come back gradually

Start lighter than before and build up over a few weeks. Your muscles return fast, but tendons and joints need time to readapt.

This article is informational and does not replace the advice of a doctor or physiotherapist, especially if your break is due to an injury.
