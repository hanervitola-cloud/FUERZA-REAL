Sleep and Recovery: Why It's as Important as Training

7 min read · Recovery
← All articles
Written by the creator of Earned Strength · Updated October 2026

You can have the perfect routine and the perfect diet, but if you sleep poorly, you're holding back your progress just as much as if you skipped the gym half the week.

In summary
  • Deep sleep is when much of the repair happens — training is only the signal
  • One night without sleep cut muscle protein synthesis by 18% in a controlled study
  • Adults need at least 7 hours; teenagers need 8–10
  • Your phone hurts sleep twice: the light delays sleepiness and the content keeps you awake
  • After a bad night, train lighter instead of chasing records — and fix the next night

Training is the stimulus — the moment you tell your body "I need to be stronger." But the real reconstruction, where that stimulus becomes muscle, happens while you rest, and especially while you sleep. Without that process, a lot of the work you put in at the gym goes to waste.

What happens in your body while you sleep

Stage What happens
Light sleepTransition, the body starts to relax
Deep sleepPeak growth hormone release, muscle tissue repair
REM sleepMemory consolidation, including motor memory from training

Deep sleep dominates the early part of the night, while REM sleep gets longer toward the morning. Cutting your night short at either end costs you something.

How much sleep you actually need

The answer depends on your age. These are the official recommendations from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine:

Age Recommended sleep per night
Teenagers (13–18)8–10 hours
Adults (18 and older)7 hours or more
What happens when athletes sleep more

In a Stanford study (Mah et al., 2011), college basketball players extended their sleep to at least 10 hours in bed per night for several weeks. Their sprint times got faster and their shooting accuracy improved. Most people who train won't need that much, but it shows how much performance can be left on the table by sleeping too little.

What happens to your progress if you sleep too little

01

Less muscle protein synthesis

In a controlled study (Lamon et al., 2021), a single night without sleep reduced muscle protein synthesis — the process that builds new muscle — by 18% in healthy young adults, even with the same diet.

02

More cortisol, less testosterone

In that same study, cortisol (a stress hormone) rose by 21% and testosterone dropped by 24%. And it doesn't take a full sleepless night: young men who slept 5 hours a night for one week saw their daytime testosterone fall by 10–15% (Leproult and Van Cauter, 2011).

03

Worse gym performance

A review in Sports Medicine (Fullagar et al., 2015) found that sleep loss tends to hurt performance — especially longer or repeated efforts — and makes the same effort feel harder. You end up training at a lower intensity without realizing it.

04

More hunger, worse food choices

When young men slept only 4 hours for two nights, their levels of leptin (the "I'm full" hormone) dropped and ghrelin (the "I'm hungry" hormone) rose, along with their hunger and appetite (Spiegel et al., 2004). That makes it much harder to stick to your diet the next day.

My experience: the nights my phone wins

These days I usually sleep 8–9 hours, which is in the recommended range. But I'd be lying if I said every night looks like that. Some nights I only get 4 or 5 hours, and the reason is almost always the same: my phone.

And I could feel it in the gym. When I slept badly, I had less strength and less desire to train at all. Not sleeping enough was one of the mistakes I made when I started (I wrote about them in the hypertrophy vs strength article), and it's the one that's easiest to fall back into.

That's why I see sleep as part of the training now, not something separate from it. The work happens at the gym, but the results get built at night.

Why your phone is the biggest sleep thief

Your phone works against your sleep in two ways. The first is light: in a study published in PNAS (Chang et al., 2015), people who read on a light-emitting screen before bed took longer to fall asleep, released less melatonin (the hormone that makes you sleepy), and felt less alert the next morning than when they read a printed book.

The second is the content itself. Videos, chats and games are designed to keep you engaged, so "five more minutes" easily becomes an hour. A meta-analysis in JAMA Pediatrics (Carter et al., 2016) found that kids and teens who used a phone or tablet at bedtime — or even just had one in the room — were more likely to sleep too little, sleep worse, and feel sleepy during the day.

01

Charge it outside your bedroom

If the phone isn't within reach, you can't pick it up "for a second." This one change removes most of the temptation.

02

Use a separate alarm clock

"I need it for my alarm" is the main excuse to keep the phone next to the bed. A cheap alarm clock takes that excuse away.

03

Set a fixed bedtime mode

Most phones can silence notifications and switch to a dim, gray screen at a set time every night. Let the phone make the decision for you.

04

If you can't sleep, don't scroll

Lying in bed with your phone usually makes it worse. Read something on paper or just rest with the lights off until you feel sleepy.

What to do after a bad night

01

Train, but don't chase records

Keep the session, but use familiar weights or do a set or two less. A bad night is the wrong day to test your max — your effort will feel harder and your technique will be worse.

02

A short nap helps

After a night of only about four hours of sleep, a 30-minute nap after lunch improved alertness and sprint performance in a study by Waterhouse et al. (2007). Keep it short so it doesn't ruin your next night.

03

Careful with extra caffeine

Coffee can get you through the morning, but caffeine lasts longer than it feels: a large dose taken 6 hours before bed still cut total sleep by more than an hour in one study (Drake et al., 2013). Keep it to the early part of the day.

04

Fix the next night, don't oversleep the next morning

Go to bed on time the following night instead of sleeping until noon. A consistent schedule is what gets your sleep back on track.

How to improve sleep quality

•

Consistent schedule

Going to bed and waking up at the same time every day (yes, including weekends) regulates your biological clock.

•

Screens off before bed

If you can, put screens away for the last 30–45 minutes before sleeping. The phone tips above make this much easier.

•

Dark and cool room

Total darkness and a cool temperature help you enter deep sleep faster and stay in it longer.

•

Watch the caffeine timing

As a simple rule, avoid caffeine in the 6–8 hours before bed, even if you feel like the effect has "worn off."

This article is informational and does not replace the advice of a doctor, especially if you have persistent insomnia or suspect a sleep disorder.
