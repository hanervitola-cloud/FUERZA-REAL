Sleep and Recovery: Why It's as Important as Training

10 min read · Recovery
← All articles
Written by the creator of Earned Strength · Updated August 2026

You can have the perfect routine and the perfect diet, but if you sleep poorly, you're holding back your progress just as much as if you skipped the gym half the week.

In summary
  • Deep sleep is where muscle repairs most — not the training itself
  • Poor sleep raises cortisol and lowers testosterone
  • 7-9 hours per night is the recommended range for people who train
  • A consistent schedule and avoiding screens before bed improve sleep quality

Training is the stimulus — the moment you tell your body "I need to be stronger." But the real reconstruction, where that stimulus becomes muscle, happens while you sleep. Without that process, the gym stimulus is largely wasted.

What happens in your body while you sleep

Stage What happens
Light sleepTransition, the body starts to relax
Deep sleepPeak growth hormone release, muscle tissue repair
REM sleepMemory consolidation, including motor memory from training

Sleep depth throughout the night

Deep sleep (where most muscle repair happens) dominates the early hours of the night — another reason why cutting sleep short from the start costs you more than waking up slightly earlier.

What happens to your progress if you sleep too little

01

Less muscle protein synthesis

With less deep sleep, the muscle repair and growth process is reduced — even if you trained and ate perfectly that day.

02

More cortisol, less testosterone

Sleep deprivation raises cortisol (stress hormone) and reduces testosterone production — a combination that makes building muscle harder and storing fat easier.

03

Worse gym performance

With insufficient sleep, strength, coordination, and reaction time all drop — you train at lower intensity without realizing it.

04

More hunger, worse food choices

Poor sleep disrupts hunger hormones (ghrelin and leptin), making you eat more and worse the next day.

How much sleep

7 to 9 hours per night is the recommended range for adults who train regularly. Consistently under 6 hours starts to measurably affect muscle recovery.

How to improve sleep quality

•

Consistent schedule

Going to bed and waking up at the same time every day (yes, including weekends) regulates your biological clock.

•

Avoid screens before bed

Blue light from your phone delays the release of melatonin, the hormone that makes you sleepy. If you can, avoid screens for the last 30-45 minutes.

•

Dark and cool room

Total darkness and a cool temperature help you enter deep sleep faster and stay in it longer.

•

Watch the caffeine timing

Caffeine can still affect your sleep up to 6-8 hours after drinking it, even if you feel like the effect has "worn off."

This article is informational and does not replace the advice of a doctor, especially if you have persistent insomnia or suspect a sleep disorder.
