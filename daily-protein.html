How Much Protein You Actually Need (Without Overdoing It)

8 min read · Nutrition
← All articles
Written by the creator of Earned Strength · Updated October 2026

Protein is, without a doubt, the most talked about — and most misunderstood — macronutrient in the training world. The supplement industry has every incentive to make you think you need far more than you actually do.

In summary
  • 1.6–2.2 g of protein per kg of bodyweight per day is the real range for building muscle
  • Eating more than that doesn't speed up muscle growth
  • In healthy people, there's no evidence it damages kidneys
  • Spreading it across 3–5 meals helps more than chasing the post-workout "window"

Between product labels, gym myths, and contradictory recommendations on social media, it's easy to end up confused about how much protein to actually eat. Here's the real number, explained with the backing of exercise science — no exaggeration in either direction.

The number by goal

The recommended protein amount isn't a single number — it changes depending on what you're trying to achieve with your body:

•

Sedentary / normal life without training

0.8 g per kg of bodyweight per day. The minimum to maintain basic body functions, with no performance goal.

•

Recreational strength training

1.4 to 1.6 g per kg of bodyweight per day. Enough to support recovery for someone training 3–4 times a week.

•

Muscle gain as the main goal

1.6 to 2.2 g per kg of bodyweight per day. This is where most of the scientific evidence sits for maximizing muscle protein synthesis in consistent trainees.

•

Caloric deficit (cutting) while training strength

2.0 to 2.4 g per kg of bodyweight per day. When you eat fewer calories, higher protein helps protect muscle while you lose fat.

Practical example

Someone weighing 70 kg training strength with a muscle-gain goal would be aiming for 112–154 g of protein per day (70 × 1.6 to 70 × 2.2).

Why "more is always better" isn't true

Past a certain point, extra protein doesn't translate into more muscle. A large meta-analysis in the British Journal of Sports Medicine (Morton et al., 2018), which pooled 49 studies, found that the benefit of extra protein on muscle gain levels off at around 1.6 g per kg of bodyweight per day — with some people benefiting up to about 2.2 g/kg. Beyond that, the leftover protein simply gets used as energy, just like any other macronutrient in excess. Eating double what you need won't make you gain muscle twice as fast.

What about the myth that it damages kidneys?

This is one of the most repeated protein myths — and one I believed myself. In healthy people with no prior kidney disease, the evidence doesn't support it:

01

The big picture

A meta-analysis in the Journal of Nutrition (Devries et al., 2018) compared healthy adults eating higher-protein diets with those eating normal or lower amounts, and found no difference in kidney function between the groups.

02

Even at very high intakes

In a one-year study (Antonio et al., 2016), resistance-trained men ate around 2.5–3.3 g per kg per day — well above the ranges in this article — with no harmful effects on markers of kidney or liver function.

The exception

If you already have a diagnosed kidney condition, the story is different: protein intake should be set by your doctor or a dietitian, not by a fitness article.

My experience: I don't count my protein

I'll be honest: I don't track how many grams of protein I eat. Most of mine comes from chicken, meat and eggs, and I don't use protein powder at all. For a long time I also believed that eating a lot of protein could hurt your kidneys — which is part of why I wanted to dig into the research above.

Not counting is fine as long as protein shows up in every meal. If you don't want to weigh food or use an app either, the table and the example day below are a simple way to check whether you're roughly in the right range.

Example: hitting your protein with chicken, meat and eggs

Here's what a day could look like for someone weighing 70 kg (target around 112–154 g), using the same foods I eat most — no protein powder needed:

Example day

About 125 g of protein

  • Breakfast: 3 eggs — about 18 g
  • Lunch: 150 g of chicken breast — about 46 g
  • Snack: 2 cups of milk — about 16 g
  • Dinner: 150 g of lean beef — about 39 g
  • The rest of your meals (rice, beans, bread, etc.) usually add a few more grams

Notice the pattern: protein in every main meal. That matters more than any single number.

Quality protein sources

Not all protein is equally complete. These are sources with a good amino acid profile:

•

Animal-based

Chicken, fish, eggs, lean beef, dairy (milk, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese) — complete protein, high bioavailability.

•

Plant-based

Lentils, chickpeas, beans, tofu, edamame, quinoa. Combining several sources throughout the day is recommended to cover all essential amino acids.

•

Supplements

Protein powder (whey or plant-based) — useful when food alone doesn't get you to your daily goal; not mandatory if your diet already covers it. The International Society of Sports Nutrition (Jäger et al., 2017) notes that whole foods can meet protein needs just as well; powder is mainly a matter of convenience.

Does the time of day matter?

Less than you'd think. A meta-analysis on protein timing (Schoenfeld, Aragon and Krieger, 2013) found that once total daily protein was accounted for, eating it right around your workout made little difference to muscle or strength gains. There's no "magic 30-minute post-workout window" that determines everything, as people believed years ago.

What does help is spreading it out. Schoenfeld and Aragon (2018) suggest aiming for roughly 0.4 g per kg of bodyweight per meal, across at least four meals — for a 70 kg person, that's about 28 g per meal. In practice: some eggs at breakfast and a solid portion of meat or chicken at lunch and dinner already gets you most of the way there.
