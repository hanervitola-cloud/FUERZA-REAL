Is Protein Powder Bad for You? What It Is and Whether You Need It

7 min read · Supplements
← All articles
Written by Haner Vitola · Updated October 2026

Protein powder gets treated either as a magic muscle builder or as something dangerous. It's neither. At its core, it's protein taken out of food and dried into a powder — so the real questions are about product quality, not about whether protein itself is bad.

In summary
  • Protein powder is protein from milk or plants, processed into a powder
  • For healthy people, normal amounts aren't harmful to the kidneys
  • The real risks are about product quality: contamination and misleading labels
  • Whey and plant proteins both work for building muscle
  • You don't need it if your food already covers your protein

What protein powder actually is

01

Whey

The liquid part of milk left over when cheese is made, filtered and dried. "Concentrate" keeps a bit more fat and lactose; "isolate" is filtered further, so it has more protein per scoop and very little lactose. It digests quickly and is rich in leucine, the amino acid most linked to muscle building.

02

Casein

The other main protein in milk. It digests slowly, which is why some people take it before bed. For muscle growth, your total daily protein matters more than which type you choose.

03

Plant protein

Usually soy, pea or rice protein, often blended together. Blends combine sources with different amino acid profiles so the final product is more complete.

Is it bad for you? What the research says

For healthy people, protein from a powder is handled by the body the same way as protein from food. The most common fear is about the kidneys, and the evidence doesn't support it: a meta-analysis in the Journal of Nutrition (Devries et al., 2018) found no difference in kidney function between healthy adults eating higher- and lower-protein diets, and a one-year study in resistance-trained men eating very high amounts of protein found no harmful effects on kidney or liver markers (Antonio et al., 2016). I break this down in more detail in the protein guide.

If you already have a kidney condition, that's different — your doctor should decide how much protein you eat.

The real risks nobody talks about

The problems with protein powder have less to do with protein and more to do with how supplements are made and sold.

01

Supplements aren't approved before they're sold

In the United States, under the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994, the FDA doesn't review supplements for safety or effectiveness before they reach the market — the manufacturer is responsible. Rules vary by country, but in many places the label is largely self-reported.

02

Contamination

When Consumer Reports tested protein drinks in 2010, it found measurable levels of heavy metals such as arsenic, cadmium and lead in some of them. Independent testing since then has continued to find heavy metals in some products, which is why third-party testing matters.

03

Inflated protein numbers

Some companies have been accused of "amino spiking": adding cheap amino acids like glycine or taurine so that a lab test counts them as protein, even though they don't build muscle the same way. If you see those near the top of the ingredient list of a protein powder, be suspicious.

04

Digestive problems

Whey concentrate contains some lactose. If dairy bothers your stomach, a whey isolate or a plant-based powder is usually easier to tolerate.

05

Banned substances

For athletes who are drug-tested, contaminated supplements are a real risk. Products certified by programs like NSF Certified for Sport or Informed Sport are tested for banned substances.

Whey or plant protein?

Whey has a slight edge on paper: it's a complete protein, rich in leucine, and digests quickly. But in practice, plant protein works too. A meta-analysis by Messina and colleagues (2018) found that supplementing with soy protein produced similar gains in muscle mass and strength as supplementing with animal protein during resistance training.

If you tolerate dairy, whey is usually the cheapest and most studied option. If you don't, or you don't eat animal products, a soy or pea-and-rice blend is a perfectly good choice.

Do you actually need it?

No — not if your food already covers your daily protein. The International Society of Sports Nutrition (Jäger et al., 2017) notes that whole foods can meet protein needs just as well; protein powder is mainly a matter of convenience.

What I do

I don't use protein powder at all. Most of my protein comes from chicken, meat and eggs — you can see a full example day in the protein guide. Powder is a tool, not a requirement.

Where it does help:

•

When you can't reach your target with food

Low appetite, a busy schedule or a tight budget for meat and fish can make one scoop a practical fix.

•

When you don't eat animal products

Plant-based eaters can find it harder to reach higher protein targets from food alone.

How to choose a good one

01

Look for third-party testing

Seals like NSF Certified for Sport or Informed Sport mean an independent lab has checked the product.

02

Check protein per scoop, not per tub

A decent whey is usually at least around 70–80% protein by weight; an isolate is closer to 90%. If a 40 g scoop only has 20 g of protein, half of what you're paying for isn't protein.

03

Keep the ingredient list short

Avoid "proprietary blends" that hide amounts, and be wary of glycine, taurine or other added amino acids in a product sold as protein.

04

Compare the price per gram of protein

Divide the price by the total grams of protein in the container. Cheaper per tub isn't always cheaper per gram.

How much, and when?

One serving of around 20–40 g of protein is typical. When you take it matters much less than your total protein for the day — after training, with breakfast, or as a snack all work. If you use it, treat it like any other protein source in your meal plan, not as something extra on top.
