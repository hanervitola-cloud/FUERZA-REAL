Signs of Overtraining: When Your Body Says Stop

6 min read · Recovery
← All articles
Written by Haner Vitola · Published Sep 24, 2026 · Updated Oct 23, 2026 · How I research

"Training more is always better" is one of the myths that causes the most injuries and plateaus. Your body sends clear signals before it breaks down. The problem is that almost nobody recognizes them in time.

In summary
  • Most gym-goers don't have true overtraining syndrome. They have too much accumulated fatigue, which is much easier to fix
  • Performance dropping instead of rising is the clearest sign of accumulated fatigue
  • Taking every set to failure doesn't build more muscle, but it does take longer to recover from
  • Normal soreness fades within about a week; pain that lasts longer deserves attention
  • A deload week (40–60% less volume) usually fixes it

What overtraining actually is

When you train, you generate fatigue; when you rest, your body recovers and adapts to be stronger than before. This is called supercompensation. Overtraining happens when you keep piling up fatigue without giving that recovery enough time, session after session, until your body can no longer keep up.

Overreaching vs. overtraining: it's a spectrum

"Overtraining" gets used for any kind of tiredness, but sports scientists separate it into three stages. A joint consensus statement from the European College of Sport Science and the American College of Sports Medicine (Meeusen et al., 2013) describes them like this:

Stage Recovery time What it means
Functional overreachingDays to about 2 weeksA normal part of hard training: after rest, you come back stronger
Non-functional overreachingWeeks to monthsToo much for too long: performance stalls or drops, with no benefit
Overtraining syndromeMonths or longerRare and serious: needs a medical evaluation to rule out other causes

For most people who lift, the real risk isn't the full syndrome. It's the middle stage: piling up fatigue week after week until progress stops and training starts to feel like a punishment. The good news is that it's preventable, and catching it early makes it much easier to fix.

Warning signs

01

Performance dropping instead of rising

If you've been moving less weight or doing fewer reps than before for several weeks, with no apparent reason, that's the clearest sign of accumulated fatigue.

02

Tiredness that sleep doesn't fix

Normal fatigue improves with a good night's sleep. Overtraining fatigue persists even after resting well.

03

Noticeable mood changes

Irritability, lack of motivation to train (even when you used to enjoy it), or anxiety related to the gym.

04

Insomnia despite being exhausted

It sounds contradictory, but sleep problems are one of the most commonly reported symptoms of overreaching. You feel completely drained and still can't sleep well.

05

Getting sick more often

Heavy training without enough recovery has been linked to more frequent colds and infections. If you're getting sick more often than usual, it may be related.

06

Persistent joint or muscle pain

Discomfort that doesn't go away with normal rest between sessions. Typical post-workout soreness peaks around 1–3 days after training and fades within about a week (Cheung et al., 2003).

Key difference

Normal post-workout tiredness improves with a couple of rest days. Overtraining lasts for weeks and affects your life outside the gym too: your mood, your sleep and your appetite.

My experience: every set to failure

I learned this one the hard way. For a long time, I did 4 sets of 12 on my exercises and took every single set to failure, with no reps left, every time. It wore me down a lot.

Once, my biceps stayed sore for two weeks after a session. That's far longer than normal soreness, and it was a clear sign I'd done much more than my body could recover from.

What I didn't know then is that the research doesn't support training like that. Going to failure on every set doesn't build more muscle than stopping a rep or two short. It mostly just makes recovery slower.

Is training to failure worth it?

Here's what the evidence says:

01

It doesn't build more muscle

A meta-analysis by Grgic et al. (2022) found no significant difference in muscle growth or strength between training to failure and stopping short of it. Another meta-analysis (Refalo et al., 2023) found that sets should end close to failure for good growth, but "close" is enough.

02

It costs more recovery

In a study by Morán-Navarro et al. (2017), people who trained to failure took longer to recover their performance (up to a day or two longer in some measures) than people who did the same work without reaching failure.

03

The practical middle ground

Stop most sets with 1–3 reps left in the tank. If you want to go to failure, save it for the last set of safer exercises like machines or curls. Avoid it on heavy squats, deadlifts or bench presses, where breaking form can get you hurt.

When soreness isn't normal

Regular soreness fades within about a week. See a doctor if the pain lasts longer than that, if it's sharp or comes with swelling, or if one muscle suddenly feels much weaker. And if your urine turns very dark (cola-colored) after an intense session, get medical help right away. It can be a sign of rhabdomyolysis, a rare but serious form of muscle breakdown.

What to do if you recognize these signs

•

Deload week

Reduce volume and intensity by 40–60% for one week, without stopping movement entirely.

•

Prioritize sleep that week

This is when good sleep has the most impact. Aim for 8+ hours if you can.

•

Don't see it as "losing progress"

A well-planned deload week doesn't set you back. It lets you come back performing better than before. Ignoring the signals can leave you sidelined for weeks or months with an injury.

How to prevent it in the first place

01

Leave reps in reserve

Ending most sets 1–3 reps short of failure gives you nearly all the growth with far less fatigue. It's the opposite of what I used to do.

02

Add volume gradually

Jumping from 10 to 25 sets a week because you saw a new routine online is how fatigue piles up. Add a set here and there, and only keep it if you're still recovering well.

03

Plan lighter weeks

Don't wait until you're exhausted. In a survey of strength and physique coaches and athletes (Bell et al., 2024), deloads were typically taken every 4–8 weeks and lasted about a week.

04

Track your performance

Write down your weights and reps. If they've been going down for two or three weeks in a row without a clear reason (like bad sleep or a cut), that's your signal to back off before it gets worse.

05

Recover outside the gym

Sleep and food are part of recovery too. Read the sleep and recovery article and make sure you're eating enough protein.
