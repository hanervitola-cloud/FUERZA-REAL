Signs of Overtraining: When Your Body Says Stop

10 min read · Recovery
← All articles
Written by the creator of Earned Strength · Updated August 2026

"Training more is always better" is one of the myths that causes the most injuries and plateaus. The body sends clear signals before it breaks down — the problem is almost nobody recognizes them in time.

In summary
  • Performance dropping instead of rising is the clearest sign of accumulated fatigue
  • Tiredness that doesn't improve even with good sleep is different from normal fatigue
  • Getting sick more often and persistent pain are also warning signs
  • A deload week (40–60% less volume) usually fixes it

What overtraining actually is

When you train, you generate fatigue; when you rest, your body recovers and adapts stronger than before — this is called supercompensation. Overtraining occurs when you keep accumulating fatigue without giving that recovery enough time, session after session, until the body can no longer keep up.

Warning signs

01

Performance dropping instead of rising

If you've been moving less weight or doing fewer reps than before for several weeks, with no apparent reason, that's the clearest sign of accumulated fatigue.

02

Tiredness that sleep doesn't fix

Normal fatigue improves with a good night's sleep. Overtraining fatigue persists even after resting well.

03

Noticeable mood changes

Irritability, lack of motivation to train (even when you used to enjoy it), or anxiety related to the gym.

04

Insomnia despite being exhausted

It sounds contradictory, but overtraining chronically raises cortisol, which can make it hard to fall asleep even when you're completely drained.

05

Getting sick more often

The immune system weakens with chronic fatigue — if you're catching colds or getting sick more frequently than usual, it may be related.

06

Persistent joint or muscle pain

Discomfort that doesn't go away with normal rest between sessions — unlike typical post-workout soreness that improves within 1–3 days.

Key difference

Normal post-workout tiredness improves with a couple of rest days. Overtraining persists for weeks and affects your life outside the gym too — mood, sleep, appetite.

What to do if you recognize these signs

•

Deload week

Reduce volume and intensity by 40–60% for one week, without stopping movement entirely.

•

Prioritize sleep that week

This is when good sleep has the most impact — aim for 8+ hours if possible.

•

Don't see it as "losing progress"

A well-executed deload week doesn't set you back — it lets you come back performing better than before. Ignoring the signals can leave you sidelined for weeks or months with an injury.
