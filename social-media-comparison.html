Why Comparing Yourself on Social Media Is Holding You Back

9 min read · Mindset
← All articles
Written by the creator of Earned Strength · Updated August 2026

You open your phone, see someone with the "perfect" physique, and in two seconds your months of progress feel meaningless. That cycle isn't an accident — and understanding it is the first step to breaking out of it.

In summary
  • What you see on social media is the best shot from hundreds of takes, not that person's daily reality
  • Many "inspirational" physiques involve exceptional genetics, years of training, or substances
  • Comparing yourself to others kills motivation faster than any bad workout
  • The only comparison that actually works is against your own past progress

What you're actually seeing

That photo or video that demotivated you isn't a spontaneous moment — it's the result of good lighting, the best angle, a pre-shoot muscle pump, editing, and sometimes filters that directly alter body shape. What you see is the most flattering 1% of that person's life, not their day-to-day reality.

01

Lighting and angle change everything

The same person can look remarkably different with overhead lighting casting deep shadows versus the flat light of a random gym mirror.

02

The pre-photo pump

It's common to train the muscle being photographed right before the shoot, so it looks fuller and more inflated than it does on a normal non-training day.

03

Temporary dehydration

Some people reduce water and carbohydrates for a day or two before a photo shoot to look more "defined" — a state that isn't sustainable or real in daily life.

04

Genetics you can't copy

Muscle insertions, bone structure, and ease of building muscle mass vary enormously between people. Someone might achieve in 2 years what takes another person 8, with the same effort.

05

Substances that go unmentioned

As covered in the steroids article, some of that "motivational" content comes from physiques with undisclosed chemical assistance — compared against that, any natural progress looks slow, even when it isn't.

Real data

Surveys within the fitness industry have shown that a considerable percentage of "shredded physique" content creators have used substances at some point — something that's almost never mentioned in the caption.

Why this holds back your real progress

Constant comparison doesn't just affect your mood — it changes training decisions. It leads to unrealistic routines copied from someone with years of head start, expectations of results in weeks that actually take years, and in the worst cases, considering dangerous shortcuts just to "catch up" to a standard that wasn't even real.

What to do instead

•

Only compare yourself to who you were 3 months ago

It's the only fair comparison that exists — same starting point, same body, same conditions.

•

Take your own progress photos

Same light, same angle, every 4-6 weeks. It's the most honest metric you'll have.

•

Curate your feed

Unfollow accounts that create anxiety instead of real motivation. There's no obligation to watch content that makes you feel bad.

•

Focus on indicators you control

Weight you lift, reps, weekly consistency — measurable things that belong to you, not a visual comparison against a stranger on the internet.

The motivation that actually lasts

Motivation that depends on feeling better than others, or on reaching someone else's physique, is fragile — it collapses the moment you see something "better." The motivation that lasts comes from a different place: seeing your own progression, feeling stronger than you were last month, and enjoying the process itself, not just the destination.

This article is informational and aims to encourage a healthier relationship with training and social media. If constant comparison is persistently affecting your mood, talking to someone you trust or a mental health professional can help.
