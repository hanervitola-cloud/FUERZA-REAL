Hypertrophy vs Strength: Differences and How to Train Each

8 min read · Training
← All articles
Written by the creator of Earned Strength · Updated October 2026

"Bigger" and "stronger" aren't the same thing, even though they're related. Training for each goal looks different — and confusing them is why many people train hard without seeing the result they're after.

In summary
  • Strength = your nervous system getting better at moving heavy weight. Hypertrophy = your muscles actually getting bigger
  • Muscle grows across a wide rep range, not just 8–12, as long as sets end close to failure
  • For size, weekly volume matters most: roughly 10–20 hard sets per muscle per week
  • Resting longer (2–3 minutes on big lifts) can mean more growth, not less
  • You can train both at the same time, with the right structure

What the evidence says

A 2021 review by Schoenfeld and colleagues found that hypertrophy occurs across a very wide rep range (from fewer than 6 to more than 20), as long as sets are taken close to muscle failure. The old myth that "only 8–12 reps make muscle grow" is outdated.

Why getting stronger isn't the same as getting bigger

When you start lifting, your strength goes up fast — sometimes from one week to the next. Most of that early jump doesn't come from bigger muscles. A classic study by Moritani and deVries found that in the first weeks of training, strength gains were explained mostly by neural adaptations: your brain learns to recruit more muscle fibers, fire them in better sync, and coordinate the movement. Real muscle growth takes longer to show up.

That's why a powerlifter and a bodybuilder can look so different even though both are strong. Strength training builds the skill of producing maximum force in a few specific lifts. Hypertrophy training builds more muscle tissue. The two overlap — a bigger muscle has more potential to be strong — but you get the most out of each one by training for it on purpose.

What actually makes a muscle grow

01

Mechanical tension

The main signal for growth is the muscle working hard against a load. Heavy and moderate weights both work, as long as the target muscle is the one being challenged — not your joints, and not momentum.

02

Enough weekly volume

A meta-analysis by Schoenfeld, Ogborn and Krieger (2017) found that more weekly sets led to more growth, with 10 or more sets per muscle per week clearly beating fewer than 5. Most people grow well with roughly 10–20 hard sets per muscle per week.

03

Getting close to failure

Sets don't need to go all the way to failure, but they need to end close to it. A 2023 meta-analysis by Refalo and colleagues found that growth tends to increase as sets end closer to failure. A practical target: stop with about 1–3 reps left in the tank.

04

Progressive overload

If you lift the same weight for the same reps forever, your body has no reason to change. Adding a rep, a little weight, or an extra set over time is what keeps the growth going.

How to train each goal

01

If you're chasing maximum strength

Prioritize the main lifts (squat, bench press, deadlift) with heavy loads of 1–6 reps, long rests of 3–5 minutes, and fewer exercises per session to allow full recovery between sets.

02

If you're chasing hypertrophy

You can use a wider range (6–20 reps), 1–3 minute rests (longer on big compound lifts), more total volume (sets per week), and take most sets close to muscle failure.

03

Can you train both at the same time?

Yes — this is called concurrent training. A common structure is to prioritize strength in compound movements at the start of the session (heavy weight, low reps), then hypertrophy in accessory exercises (more reps, more volume).

Rest periods: the mistake most people make

A lot of people think short rests are better for size because the pump feels bigger. The evidence points the other way. In a study on trained men, Schoenfeld and colleagues (2016) compared resting 1 minute versus 3 minutes between sets: the 3-minute group gained more strength and showed more muscle growth in several measurements. The likely reason is simple — with more rest, you can do more quality reps on the next set, and that adds up to more total work.

You don't need to time 3 minutes for every curl. A good rule: rest 2–3 minutes on big compound lifts (squats, presses, rows) and 1–2 minutes on smaller isolation exercises.

What I actually do (and the mistakes I made first)

I've been training for about four years, and my goal has always been size, not maximum strength. Most of my sets are in the 8–12 rep range, which sits right in the middle of the hypertrophy spectrum above: heavy enough to get stronger along the way, light enough to control the weight and get enough volume in.

I train three days a week with a simple split: one day for the full lower body, one day for back and biceps, and one day for chest, shoulders and triceps.

An honest note about my split

With this setup, each muscle gets trained once a week. Research suggests that total weekly volume matters more than how many days you spread it over (Schoenfeld, Grgic and Krieger, 2019), so it works. But if you have more days available, splitting the same sets across two sessions per muscle can make it easier to keep every set high quality.

Getting here wasn't clean. When I started, I made pretty much every classic mistake:

01

Too much weight, bad technique

I loaded the bar for my ego, not for the muscle. The weight moved, but the muscle I wanted to grow wasn't doing most of the work — and that's how people get hurt.

02

Switching routines all the time

I jumped from one program to another before any of them had time to work. Muscle growth is slow; no routine can prove itself in two weeks.

03

Not adding weight

I kept using the same weights without writing anything down, so there was no progressive overload — the one thing that forces the body to adapt.

04

Not eating or sleeping enough

I trained hard but didn't give my body what it needed to recover. Without enough food, protein and sleep, a lot of that effort in the gym goes to waste.

What I'd tell anyone starting out: pick a sensible routine, stick with it, write down your weights and reps, and try to beat them little by little. Eat enough protein and get your sleep. It's boring advice — and it's exactly what works.

Example: a 3-day hypertrophy split

This is the same structure I follow. The exercises are common examples, so swap them for whatever your gym has. Aim for sets of 8–12 reps, stop 1–3 reps short of failure, and try to add a rep or a bit of weight over time.

Day 1

Legs

  • Squat or leg press — 3–4 sets
  • Romanian deadlift — 3 sets
  • Leg curl — 3 sets
  • Leg extension — 3 sets
  • Calf raises — 3–4 sets
Day 2

Back and biceps

  • Pull-ups or lat pulldown — 3–4 sets
  • Barbell or dumbbell row — 3–4 sets
  • Seated cable row — 3 sets
  • Biceps curl — 3 sets
  • Hammer curl — 2–3 sets
Day 3

Chest, shoulders and triceps

  • Bench press — 3–4 sets
  • Incline dumbbell press — 3 sets
  • Overhead press — 3 sets
  • Lateral raises — 3 sets
  • Triceps pushdown — 3 sets

That works out to roughly 6–10 direct sets per week for the bigger muscle groups (fewer for smaller ones like the triceps, which also work during every press), which is on the lower end of the range above. If your progress stalls, adding a set or two per exercise is the first thing to try.

Quick answers to common questions

01

Do I need to lift heavy to get bigger?

No. Heavy weights work, but so do moderate and lighter ones, as long as the sets end close to failure. Lifting very heavy matters most if your goal is maximum strength.

02

Will high reps just make me "toned"?

"Toned" usually means having some muscle and low enough body fat to see it. High-rep sets taken close to failure still build muscle; whether you look toned or big depends mostly on how much muscle you have and your body fat level.

03

Will I get stronger if I only train for size?

Yes. More muscle gives you more potential strength, and 8–12 rep training will make you stronger too. You just won't get as good at 1–3 rep maxes as someone who practices them regularly.

The common thread: mechanical tension

Both strength and hypertrophy depend on generating enough mechanical tension in the muscle. The difference is in how that tension is achieved — with very heavy loads and few reps (strength), or with sets taken close to failure across a wider rep range (hypertrophy). Neither path works without progressive load increases over time.
