Hypertrophy vs Strength: Differences and How to Train Each

9 min read · Training
← All articles
Written by the creator of Earned Strength · Updated August 2026

"Bigger" and "stronger" aren't the same thing, even though they're related. Training for each goal looks different — and confusing them is why many people train hard without seeing the result they're after.

In summary
  • Strength = improving the nervous system to move more weight. Hypertrophy = increasing muscle size
  • Recent research shows hypertrophy happens across a wide rep range, not just 8–12
  • Strength needs heavier loads and longer rest between sets
  • You can train both at the same time, with the right structure

What the evidence says

A meta-analysis by Schoenfeld et al. (2021) found that hypertrophy occurs across a very wide rep range (from fewer than 6 to more than 20), as long as sets are taken close to muscle failure. The old myth that "only 8–12 reps make muscle grow" is outdated.

How to train each goal

01

If you're chasing maximum strength

Prioritize the main lifts (squat, bench press, deadlift) with heavy loads of 1–6 reps, long rests of 3–5 minutes, and fewer exercises per session to allow full recovery between sets.

02

If you're chasing hypertrophy

You can use a wider range (6–20 reps), 1–2 minute rests, more total volume (sets per week), and take most sets close to muscle failure.

03

Can you train both at the same time?

Yes — this is called concurrent training. A common structure is to prioritize strength in compound movements at the start of the session (heavy weight, low reps), then hypertrophy in accessory exercises (more reps, more volume).

The common thread: mechanical tension

Both strength and hypertrophy depend on generating sufficient mechanical tension in the muscle. The difference is in how that tension is achieved — with very heavy loads and few reps (strength), or with sets taken close to failure across a wider rep range (hypertrophy). Neither path works without progressive load increases over time.
