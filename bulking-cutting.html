Bulking vs Cutting: How to Know Which Phase to Be In

7 min read · Nutrition
← All articles
Written by the creator of Earned Strength · Updated October 2026

"Am I bulking or cutting?" is one of the most common questions at the gym — and the right answer depends on where you stand right now, not what everyone else is doing.

In summary
  • Bulk: eating in a surplus to gain muscle. Cut: a deficit to lose fat. Recomp: around maintenance, doing a bit of both
  • Aim to gain about 0.25–0.5% of your body weight per week in a bulk, and lose 0.5–1% per week in a cut
  • Beginners, people coming back from a break, and people with more body fat can often build muscle and lose fat at the same time
  • The most common mistake is staying in one phase for years without cycling

What each phase is

01

Bulk

Eating in a caloric surplus (more calories than you burn) to maximize muscle gain. Gaining some fat is normal — the key is that most of the gain is muscle.

02

Cut

Eating in a caloric deficit (fewer calories than you burn) to lose fat while trying to preserve as much muscle as possible.

03

Recomposition (the option people forget)

Eating around maintenance, or in a small deficit, with high protein and hard training, to build muscle and lose fat at the same time. It's slower than a dedicated bulk or cut, but for some people it's the smartest choice — more on that below.

The full cycle

How phases alternate over time

It's not a one-and-done linear process — most people cycle between these phases multiple times over their years of training.

How to know which phase to be in

•

If you're new to training (less than 1 year) and fairly lean

A small surplus usually makes sense — this is when the most muscle can be gained. If you're new but already carry a fair amount of body fat, recomposition at maintenance or a small deficit is often the better start.

•

If you feel you have more fat than you'd like

If you struggle to see muscle definition even in good light, or clothes fit noticeably tighter than a few months ago, it's probably time for a cutting phase.

•

If you feel very lean or low on energy

If you've been in a deficit for a while, feel low energy, or gym performance has dropped noticeably, that's a signal to move to bulk or at least maintenance.

Simple reference

If you can't see even a little ab definition in good light without flexing, you'd probably benefit from a cutting phase before continuing to bulk.

How fast should you gain or lose?

Speed is where most people go wrong. Gaining weight fast doesn't build muscle faster — the extra mostly turns into fat. And losing weight fast makes it much harder to keep the muscle you already have.

01

When bulking: about 0.25–0.5% of body weight per week

A review on off-season nutrition for natural lifters (Iraki et al., 2019) recommends a slow rate of gain in this range. Beginners can aim for the higher end, since they build muscle faster; more experienced lifters should stay closer to the lower end.

02

When cutting: about 0.5–1% of body weight per week

That's the range recommended for natural lifters by Helms et al. (2014). Going slower pays off: in a study of elite athletes (Garthe et al., 2011), both groups lost about the same amount of weight, but the group losing about 0.7% per week lost more fat and actually gained lean mass, while the group losing about 1.4% per week gained none.

Example for a 70 kg person

Bulking: roughly 0.2–0.35 kg per week (about 0.7–1.4 kg per month). Cutting: roughly 0.35–0.7 kg per week. Weigh yourself a few mornings a week and look at the weekly average, not single days — water and food can move the scale by a kilo from one day to the next.

To find your starting calories, estimate your maintenance with the protein and calorie calculator, then adjust up or down based on what the weekly average actually does over two or three weeks.

Recomposition: when you don't need to bulk or cut

For years, the standard advice was that you can't gain muscle and lose fat at the same time. That's not quite true. A review in the Strength and Conditioning Journal (Barakat et al., 2020) concluded that body recomposition is possible, even in people with training experience, as long as they lift hard and eat enough protein.

It's most likely to work for three groups of people:

01

Beginners

The body responds so strongly to a new training stimulus that it can build muscle even without a calorie surplus.

02

People coming back after a break

Muscle you've built before comes back faster than it took to build the first time, so a big surplus usually isn't needed to regain it. I explain why in the discipline and motivation article.

03

People with more body fat

Stored body fat can cover part of the energy cost of building muscle, which makes building muscle in a small deficit more realistic.

The trade-off is speed: recomposition is slower, and the scale may barely move even while your body changes. Progress photos, how your clothes fit, and your strength in the gym are better signs than your weight.

My take: I've never done a formal bulk or cut

I'll be upfront: I've never done a structured bulk or cut, and I've never counted calories. That's more common than social media makes it look — you don't need a perfect nutrition plan to make progress, especially in your first years of training.

And right now, coming back after having to stop because of injuries, I'm in exactly the situation where recomposition makes the most sense. That's why I wanted this article to cover all three options, not just the two everyone talks about.

How to bulk without gaining too much fat

01

Keep the surplus small

A few hundred calories above maintenance is enough. "Eating everything in sight" mostly adds fat that you'll have to cut later.

02

Track the weekly average

If you're gaining faster than the range above, eat a little less. If the scale hasn't moved in two or three weeks, eat a little more.

03

Make the extra food count

Get enough protein every day (see the protein guide) and build the rest of the surplus mostly from normal meals, not just sweets and fast food.

04

Train with progressive overload

A surplus only becomes muscle if you give it a reason to. Without hard training, the extra calories just become fat.

How to cut without losing muscle

01

Keep lifting heavy

The training that built your muscle is what tells your body to keep it. Don't switch to only light weights and high reps during a cut.

02

Raise your protein

Protein needs go up in a deficit. Helms et al. (2014) recommend higher intakes during a cut specifically to protect lean mass.

03

Don't rush it

Stay within 0.5–1% of body weight per week. Losing faster usually means losing more muscle along with the fat.

04

Protect your sleep

Short sleep makes you hungrier and hurts muscle repair, which is the opposite of what you need in a cut. See the sleep and recovery article.

The most common mistake

Spending years in "permanent bulk" without ever cutting, accumulating more and more fat — or the opposite, staying in a deficit for months on end chasing a "perfect" physique and losing muscle in the process. Both phases have their time — the key is not getting stuck in either one indefinitely.

A related mistake is jumping straight from one extreme to the other. A common approach is to spend a few weeks at maintenance between phases, so your body, your appetite and your training can settle before you change direction.
