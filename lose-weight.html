How to Actually Lose Weight (Without Extreme Diets)

5 min read · Nutrition
← All articles
Written by Haner Vitola · Published Sep 24, 2026 · Updated Oct 23, 2026 · How I research

If you came here tired of feeling uncomfortable in your own body, of clothes not fitting the way you'd like, or of not recognizing yourself in the mirror, I want to start with something simple: you're not alone, and it's not a lack of willpower. Most of the time, the problem isn't the person. It's that they've been sold wrong information or diets that are impossible to stick to.

In summary
  • Losing weight depends on consistently eating less energy than you burn. Nothing more, nothing less
  • A rate of 0.5% to 1% of your bodyweight per week is realistic and sustainable
  • Strength training is just as important as food, because it keeps you from losing muscle along with fat
  • Extreme diets usually fail not from lack of discipline, but because they're impossible to sustain long-term

This article doesn't promise an overnight transformation or a magic trick. What I can give you is what actually works, explained clearly, so you can build the body and health you want at a pace you can genuinely sustain.

Why it's so hard (and why it's not your fault)

Losing weight is, at its core, simple in theory: burn more energy than you take in. But "simple" doesn't mean "easy." We're surrounded by food engineered to be irresistible, schedules that leave little time to cook, and an industry that sells restrictive diets that ignore how hunger, stress, and real daily life actually work. If you've tried before and it didn't work, the most likely explanation is that the plan wasn't built to last, not that you failed.

The only thing that really matters: the caloric deficit

Every weight-loss method that works, whether it's called keto, intermittent fasting, or the "military diet," works for the same reason: it puts you in a calorie deficit one way or another. There's no shortcut around that rule. What does change between methods is how easy they are to stick with, and that's the real difference between a good one and a bad one for you.

You can calculate a reasonable starting point with the calorie and protein calculator. It gives you a reference based on your weight, activity level, and goal, instead of a generic number that ignores your situation.

Rate of loss % of bodyweight per week How sustainable is it?
Very aggressive More than 1.5% Hard to sustain, higher risk of losing muscle
Recommended 0.5% – 1% Sustainable, good balance between speed and adherence
Conservative Less than 0.5% Very sustainable, ideal if you want to protect muscle to the maximum

For example, if you weigh 80 kg, a recommended rate would be losing between 400g and 800g per week. It looks slow compared to what any ad promises, but it's exactly what makes the result stick afterward.

What almost nobody tells you

01

It's not just about eating less, it's about eating better

Two people with the same caloric deficit can feel completely different if one eats mostly protein, vegetables, and filling foods, while the other just cuts calories any way they can. Satiety matters just as much as the final number.

02

Lifting weights is not optional

If you lose weight with diet and cardio alone, a portion of what you lose will be muscle, not just fat. Strength training during the process tells your body to preserve muscle and prioritize burning fat. If you're starting out, my beginner routine is a good starting point.

03

Sleep affects this more than you think

Poor sleep disrupts the hormones that regulate hunger and fullness, making eating at a deficit feel much harder than it needs to be. You can read more in my article on sleep and recovery.

04

There will be weeks the scale doesn't move

Your weight goes up and down because of water retention, hormonal cycles, stress, and other factors that have nothing to do with actual fat. That's normal, and it doesn't mean something is failing. Look at the trend over several weeks, not the number on one day.

Signs a plan is too extreme

If any of this sounds familiar about a plan you're following or considering, it's time to rethink it:

  • Bans entire food groups without a real medical reason
  • Promises more than 1.5% of your bodyweight per week on a sustained basis
  • Tells you constant hunger is "part of the process" and you just have to push through it
  • Depends on a specific product or supplement to "work"
  • Makes you feel guilt for eating something off-plan once in a while

If you see yourself in several of these signs in your relationship with food, regardless of any specific plan, it's worth talking to a health professional. A dietitian or doctor can help you far more than any article, including this one.

The part that doesn't depend only on food

None of this works if you feel like you're at war with yourself every time you look in the mirror. Losing weight shouldn't be the price you pay to deserve to feel good about yourself. You deserve that already, while you work toward the body and health you want. Consistency pays off far more when it comes from taking care of yourself, not punishing yourself. My article on discipline and motivation goes deeper into how to keep going without needing to feel inspired every single day.

This article is informational and does not replace the advice of a doctor or nutritionist, especially if you have a weight-related health condition, a history of an eating disorder, or are under 18.
